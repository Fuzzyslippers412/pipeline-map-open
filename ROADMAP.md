# Door Notes: the next three releases

The useful test is personal: can someone name a person who opened access for Black workers, find the record, and tell how far it reaches toward the job they have today?

## Career map release · One career map, starting with project managers

**Reader outcome.** A Black worker can enter a real title in any field, find a person or institution whose record speaks to that work, understand what changed, and take away one useful question. A field milestone, an example of similar duties and a verified connection to the reader’s employer each need their own evidence. The shared map, project manager history and reviewed field guides are implemented. The five-worker reader study is still pending.

### 1. Fix the project manager result before expanding the directory

The shipped resolver offers three database labels for “project manager.” Engineering is first, followed by a catch-all and IT. The general Project Management Specialists occupation is missing from those choices. Plain “PM” and the suggestion “Project Manager (PM)” resolve only to IT. The quoted engineering result is reproducible through the first choice. A database alias describes a possible match and supplies no historical evidence.

Use “Project manager” as the working title and offer “What kinds of projects do you lead?” with construction or design, technology, healthcare or research, business or operations, community programs, and “Across fields / skip.” Let readers see the general PM record immediately. For the initials alone, confirm the title first (including product manager, program manager, and another meaning). Retain the exact typed title, workplace and answers for editing within the tab. Use Project Management Specialists as the occupational reference for general coordination and check sector-specific duties before assigning another occupation. Leading a project does not imply personnel management. [O*NET occupational tasks](https://www.onetonline.org/link/details/13-1082.00)

## Pilot: explore roles and the doors people opened

Offer two starting points on the homepage: **Trace my job** and **Explore what I could do**. Both lead to the same public workplace records, historical sources and open questions.

**1. Show responsibility alongside the kind of work.** Start with IT support and systems work. Let readers browse by learning on the job, doing the work independently, leading technical work, supervising a team, managing managers or departments, and directing an organization. Allow “not sure” and movement sideways. Keep the job title the reader entered. For ambiguous titles, ask one question about what they oversee. “Senior support specialist” can be an experienced practitioner. For “network manager,” ask whether the person manages infrastructure, people or both. Use local job descriptions, agreements or promotion standards for an employer-specific grade. Otherwise label the grade unknown.

Show two possible directions from computer support: specialize in networks or systems, or supervise a support team. For each, compare day-to-day responsibilities, what a worker can learn next, who controls entry, and the source supporting the move. Put regional training, pay, costs and schedules on a card only when a dated source covers that place and role. A national career transition cannot establish a promotion policy at someone's workplace. [BLS on advancement from computer support](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/computer-support-specialists.htm#tab-4)

**2. Start a youth path with an activity.** Pilot with middle and high school students. Ask separately, “What do people already ask you for help with?” and “What would you like to try, even if you haven’t had a chance yet?” Allow choices like fixing, explaining, caring, designing, investigating and organizing. Show two or three possibilities with a one-sentence explanation tied to published job tasks. Let the student change an answer or explore another field. No score, account or school name is needed. “I help my aunt with her phone” plus “I like explaining things” could lead to user support and technical writing. Try writing three troubleshooting steps on paper and asking someone to follow them. [O*NET user support tasks](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/15-1232.00) · [O*NET technical writing tasks](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/27-3042.00)

**3. Give each suggestion a Door Note.** Name the person, what changed for Black workers, the record they left, where the evidence stops, and one thing a reader can try. For computing, Joseph Thompson’s oral history gives a young reader an operator’s own account of entering MIT’s Whirlwind project in 1951. The modern support or writing role remains a separate possibility. In nursing, Anna B. Coles offers a different door to investigate: who could lead a nursing school and help train the next class. Pair frontline workers’ accounts with records about decisions on training, supervision and hiring. If community members contribute career advice later, publish it with their consent and credit it as first-person advice. [Thompson’s museum record](https://computerhistory.org/stories/trailblazers-and-change-agents/#joethompson) · [Howard nursing path](https://doornotes.org/preview/p/registered-nurse/?example=1#path-preview)

**4. Test relevance before expanding.** Walk through the phone pilot with five Black workers across support, specialist and supervisory roles, and separately with five young people and input from a counselor. Ask workers which next move fits their actual work and which local rule is unknown. Ask students why two jobs appeared, whose history they found and which activity they would try. Rework any screen that readers interpret as a promised promotion or a prediction. Advance when at least four of five in each group can explain their options and choose a useful next step without help. Include a paper-only activity. These are proposed tests; no participant outcomes have been collected for this pilot.

The pilot now includes the [activity explorer](https://doornotes.org/explore/) and [IT career map](https://doornotes.org/career-map/). Use the [reader exercises](https://doornotes.org/try/routes/) to run the proposed sessions.

## What a reader can take away
Workers can follow a computing match into a relevant guide, compare a technical and a people-management direction, and copy or print a conversation note. The note carries its occupational sources and a question about what still needs verification at the reader’s workplace. The student explorer now has five individual job activity cards. A shared card contains only a public job identifier.

A citation to the source register now opens that record on its own, with the table link, the population definition and a row locator where available. The rest of the register can be filtered by profession and publisher or title. Next reader test: can someone get from their own title to a question they would actually ask, or from an activity to a record they would share? The worker and student exercises above ask both. Real participant results are still needed.

## Matching the job people actually type

One reader reported trouble tracing DevOps, site reliability, IT, and systems administration. On November 3, 2026, the live index returned zero professions for “devops eng”, “Site Reliability engineer”, and “system admin”. “IT” returned six unrelated profession cards. The job form suggested “Site Reliability Engineer”, then displayed “No field matched this job title” after selection. The index found the software card for “software Eng”, while the submitted abbreviation alone did not resolve.

**1. Use one title resolver.** Keep the occupation codes, full titles, abbreviations, and source from the two O*NET files already imported. Retain every occupation associated with each title. The current 48,330-string autocomplete list drops those relationships. For example, O*NET lists DevOps Engineer under Software Developers, but Site Reliability Engineer under three occupations. Its short title “SRE” can also mean Software Requirements Engineer. These are classification facts, separate from historical evidence. Use the same resolver for the profession index, suggestions, and the submitted job form, including a compact local fallback for common titles. Keep the reader’s original title and exact workplace separate from the selected occupation. [O*NET job-title schema](https://www.onetcenter.org/dictionary/31.0/json/job_titles.html)

**2. Give readers a useful result at each level of coverage.** An exact, supported job opens its history. A recognized occupation with only related history shows the occupation, labels the available history, and lets the reader choose whether to open it. A broad term prompts one question about duties. An uncovered title stays on screen with an explicit research gap and the existing workplace-source search. Only attach an employer milestone or a workforce chart when its own evidence supports the occupation and scope shown.

| Reader types | Expected experience |
| --- | --- |
| devops eng / DevOps engineer | Recognize DevOps, retain the reader’s wording, and identify any reviewed computing history as related unless the event specifically names the specialty. |
| Site Reliability engineer / SRE | Recognize the full title. O*NET associates it with several occupations. Ask about the work if the distinction changes the suggested path. For the initials, also allow other meanings. |
| system admin / sysadmin | Recognize the shorthand and keep the title visible. If needed, ask whether they manage systems and networks or primarily administer databases. Both occupations occur under the O*NET alias. |
| IT / regular IT | Ask which type of work they do: support people, run systems or networks, build software, secure systems, or something else. |
| software Eng / software engineer | Open the existing software engineering path by typing, submitting, or selecting the suggestion. |
| security engineer / software engineer recruiter | Clarify digital versus physical security when the title alone is ambiguous. A recruiting title must never inherit a software engineering trace on word overlap alone. |

**3. Research the first missing IT paths.** Start with systems administration and software reliability. Identify a named Black worker, training program, or institutional decision with a primary source. Label broader computing history with its actual scope. Keep links to a particular specialty and workplace open until a record establishes them. One starting point is Joseph Thompson operating MIT’s Whirlwind computer in 1951. Use it as a documented example of Black work in computer operations. A connection to a modern SRE role or a particular employer needs its own source. [Computer History Museum account and oral history](https://computerhistory.org/stories/trailblazers-and-change-agents/)

**Release gate.** Use the table as a fixture. Verify raw submission, keyboard selection, tapping a suggestion, and the index on a phone. All four reported misses must lead to an appropriate recognized role or a useful choice. No unrelated profession may be silently selected. Check that known roles such as registered nurse and electrician still resolve and that an unavailable autocomplete index leaves the typed title usable. Re-run the tester’s phrases with the tester after release. Record outcomes for the fixed fixtures and voluntarily shared test responses without logging visitors’ raw searches.

The title resolver, duty choices, and computing operations record have been implemented. The next reader check is to repeat the reported searches on a phone and ask whether the reader can distinguish a field record from a documented workplace connection.
